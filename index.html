Safe water, filtered through the coconut.

An eight-stage filter cartridge built around cellulose extracted from coconut husk. It combines physical filtration, adsorption, silver-based antimicrobial treatment and mineral exchange in a single 10-inch unit, without the pumps and energy bills of reverse osmosis.

Salty, contaminated water shouldn't need a power plant to fix.

Across coastal and rural regions, saline intrusion and groundwater contamination make everyday drinking water unsafe, and treatment infrastructure is often out of reach.

Reverse osmosis and thermal desalination work, but they depend on high pressure, steady electricity and ongoing operating costs that many households and communities can't sustain.

We set out to build a decentralised filter from low-cost, locally available materials, starting with the coconut husk that coastal communities already have in abundance.

Eight layers, one direction of flow.

Water enters at the top and passes through each stage in sequence. Select a stage to find it in the cartridge, drag any layer up, down or sideways to pull it out, or pull the cartridge apart to see every layer.

    Four mechanisms working in sequence.

    Each layer is chosen for what it removes or adds, so the water is treated in stages rather than by one material doing everything.

    Physical filtration

    PVA non-woven sheets and a fine cellulose matrix trap sediment, suspended matter and carbon fines.

    Adsorption

    Two beds of activated carbon capture chlorine, organic contaminants and taste and odour compounds.

    Desalination and antimicrobial

    The silver-functionalised MCC layer adsorbs saline ions and heavy metals, and its Ag⁺ ions inhibit bacterial growth within the filter bed.

    Mineral and ion exchange

    Mineral stone adds calcium and magnesium, while zeolite and silica exchange and adsorb selected dissolved metals.

    From agricultural waste to filter media.

    Coconut husk is usually discarded or burned. Its fibres are rich in cellulose, which we process into microcrystalline cellulose (MCC), a fine, high-surface-area material that forms the heart of our desalination layer. Silver-functionalised MCC adsorbs saline ions and heavy metals while also inhibiting microbial growth.

    Using a local by-product keeps the cartridge affordable, makes replacement media easier to source, and turns waste into something useful.

    Claims we will prove before we make them.

    Multiple filtration layers do not automatically mean desalination. Substantial salt removal needs an ion-selective mechanism, so every cartridge design is being tested against these six measures before we state its performance.

    Total dissolved solids

    mg/L

    The headline measure of desalination. WHO considers water below about 600 mg/L to taste good.

    Electrical conductivity

    µS/cm

    A fast proxy for dissolved ions, tracked before and after filtration.

    Sodium and chloride

    Na⁺ / Cl⁻, mg/L

    Direct measurement of salt ions, the real test of any desalination claim.

    pH

    6.5 – 8.5 target

    Confirms the mineral stage keeps water within the acceptable drinking range.

    Turbidity

    NTU

    Shows how well the physical layers remove suspended particles and fines.

    Microbial quality

    E. coli, CFU / 100 mL

    Verifies the silver-cellulose layer's antimicrobial effect. Safe water has none detected.

    Current status: the filtration, adsorption and antimicrobial stages are designed on established principles. Desalination performance will only be claimed once laboratory results for TDS, conductivity and Na⁺/Cl⁻ are available.
